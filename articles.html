A direct analysis of comparatives in Lithuanian

When you hear the sentence “John is taller than Tom,” “than Tom” is understood as than Tom is tall. In this paper I contribute to a lively debate in linguistics: is Tom just a single word after than or whether it is a shortened version of a hidden sentence “than Tom is tall”. Lithuanian has two words for than and I show that one of them, už selects a plain noun. I also found a new pattern in these sentences: the thing being compared has to be the subject. Thus while in English John ate more doughnuts than cookies is a possible sentence, in Lithuanian it isn’t with už. The data discussed in this paper matter beyond Lithuanian, because it weakens a test linguists have used to detect hidden structures in many languages.

Negative events (with Aleksandre Maskharashvili and Symon Stevens-Guille)

This paper contrasts existential constructions There are cats here and locative constructions The cats are here. These constructions are built very similarly in English. In Serbian and Lithuanian, however, they are built very differently. In the existential construction “There are cats here”, the verb are stops agreeing with the cats, and the cats switch to a special form (the genitive, roughly of the cats). Earlier work treated this as the verb falling back on a default setting. With my co-author Alberto Frasson, we argue that there is an invisible placeholder subject that the verb agrees with. The clue is Serbian past tense, where the verb comes out neuter singular, exactly what an invisible “it” would trigger, so it isn't default. The same account explains both languages and needs fewer specia-purpose tools than earlier proposals.
